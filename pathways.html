Pathways

Which road is yours?

There isn't one single route to U.S. citizenship, which trips up a lot of people early on. Almost everybody comes through permanent residence first, but how you get there and how long you end up waiting really does depend on your own situation. Have a look for the one that sounds closest to you.

Read this first. Nearly every pathway below ends up in the same place: you become a lawful permanent resident (a green card holder), you wait out the required years, and then you file Form N-400 to naturalize. What really changes from pathway to pathway is how you reach permanent residence in the first place, since that last stretch looks pretty similar for almost everybody.

The final step, for almost everyone

Once you're a permanent resident, naturalization asks for all of the things below. If you're short on one of them, that usually just means waiting a while longer before you file.

Time as a resident

Five years, or three if you're married to a U.S. citizen and still living with them.

Continuous residence

You've kept the U.S. as your home. Trips of six months or more can break this, so keep track of them.

Physical presence

Actually here for at least half the period — so 30 months out of five years, or 18 out of three.

Good moral character

This goes well beyond a criminal record. Unfiled taxes and unpaid child support both count against you.

English and civics

Basic English is enough; nobody's expecting fluency. There's also the civics test, with exemptions for age and disability.

The Oath of Allegiance

The real finish line, and the one people forget about — you aren't a citizen until you've taken it.

A couple of smaller requirements on top of that: you need to be at least 18 when you file, and you need to have lived three months or more in the state or USCIS district where you're applying.

Six common routes

Find the one that sounds like you

1

If you already have a green card

The five-year route

This is the most common pathway and probably the most straightforward one here. You already have permanent residence, so from this point it's mostly a question of time passing and paperwork being in order.

  • Count five years from the "resident since" date on your green card.
  • Reconstruct every trip abroad. Any absence of six months or more needs evidence that you kept your home here.
  • Sort out taxes, child support, and Selective Service registration before you file, not after.
  • File Form N-400 — you may file up to 90 days early.
  • Biometrics, interview, tests, decision, oath.

2

If you are married to a U.S. citizen

The three-year route

Marriage to a citizen cuts the wait down from five years to three, though it does add a few conditions along the way, and the marriage has to be genuine and still going.

  • Your spouse must have been a U.S. citizen for the full three years.
  • You must have been living in marital union with them for those three years, and still be living together when you naturalize.
  • If your green card was conditional (a marriage under two years old), you must remove the conditions with Form I-751 first.
  • Expect to document the marriage thoroughly — joint finances, shared address, photographs over time.
  • If the marriage ends before you naturalize, you generally revert to the five-year route rather than losing eligibility.
3

If you are a child, or a parent of one

Children: often citizens already

Honestly, this is the pathway people miss most often. Plenty of children become citizens automatically, with no application and no test at all, and the only thing left to do is get hold of the paperwork that proves it.

Born in the United States
Then you are a citizen. This was challenged in 2025, and the Supreme Court upheld it 6–3 in June 2026. Your birth certificate is the proof.
Automatic citizenship, INA 320
A child under 18 who is a permanent resident, living in the U.S. in the legal and physical custody of a parent who is a U.S. citizen, generally becomes a citizen automatically.
Documenting it afterwards
If that applies, there is nothing to naturalize. You apply for a Certificate of Citizenship on Form N-600, or simply get a U.S. passport, to document something that is already true.
Born abroad to citizen parents
Children in this position may have citizenship from birth, documented through a Consular Report of Birth Abroad.

Worth checking: adults sometimes discover they have been citizens since childhood and never knew.

4

If you came as a refugee or were granted asylum

The humanitarian route

Refugees and asylees reach citizenship through permanent residence, with one valuable advantage in how the clock is counted.

Getting the green card
Apply with Form I-485. Refugees are expected to after one year, and asylees become eligible after one year.
The advantage worth knowing about
A refugee's permanent residence is normally backdated to the date of arrival, and an asylee's by a year. That can leave you eligible to naturalize sooner than you would expect, so check the "resident since" date printed on your card.
Bringing family
Form I-730 does this, and there is a two-year deadline running from your grant of status.
Public charge
These rules generally do not apply to refugees and asylees.
Then the usual route
The standard five-year path, counted from that backdated date.
5

If you have served in the U.S. military

The service route

Military service comes with the most generous naturalization provisions anywhere in immigration law, and they go underused all the time, which is a shame.

Peacetime service, INA 328
One year of honorable service can qualify you, and the residence requirement is reduced.
Designated hostilities, INA 329
Even a single day of honorable active-duty service during a designated period can qualify you, and this route does not require a green card at all.
What to file
Form N-400, together with Form N-426, the certification of military service.
Cost
There is no filing fee for military naturalization applicants.
Family
Spouses and children of service members have provisions of their own, including expedited processing in some circumstances.

Your installation's legal assistance office can help at no cost.

6

If you have no green card yet

Getting to permanent residence first

If you aren't a permanent resident yet, then that's really your next step, and citizenship comes a good while after. Here are the common routes:

Family
A citizen spouse, parent, or adult child, or a permanent resident spouse or parent. Worth knowing: a U.S. citizen child who has turned 21 can petition for a parent, which is probably the most overlooked option among people who have been here a long time.
Employment
An employer sponsors you, usually through Form I-140. The waits vary enormously by category and by country of birth.
Asylum
If you fear persecution at home. There is a general one-year filing deadline from arrival, though it does have exceptions.
U and T visas
For victims of certain crimes, and of human trafficking.
VAWA self-petition
If you have been abused by a citizen or resident spouse, parent, or adult child. You can file this without their knowledge or involvement at all.
The Diversity Visa lottery
For nationals of eligible countries. Entering is free, so anyone charging you for it is defrauding you.

Before you act

Three things we want to be straight with you about

  • This page is a map, and maps aren't advice

    We can tell you which pathways exist and what each one generally asks for. What we can't do is tell you which one applies to your case, since that turns on details of your own history that really do need an attorney to assess. If anything in your record is complicated — an old arrest, a prior removal order, time out of status, a denied application — please talk to a lawyer before you file anything at all.

  • Leaving the country is sometimes the actual risk

    For anyone who has built up unlawful presence, simply departing the U.S. can trigger three- or ten-year bars on coming back. So "just go and apply from home" turns out to be catastrophic advice fairly often, and you shouldn't travel on the strength of a web page, this one very much included.

  • Nobody can sell you a faster pathway

    There's no way to pay for faster naturalization, and entering the Diversity Visa lottery is free, so anyone charging you for it is running a scam. A notario, despite the name, doesn't carry anything like the authority of a U.S. lawyer. And if someone guarantees you an outcome, they're lying — even genuine representatives can't promise results.
